Pedal-Powered Electricity Generation System

the problem

Design a bicycle-mounted system that converts human pedaling into stored electrical energy — able to charge a 12V battery and small devices, safe at variable pedaling speeds, and portable enough to move between different bicycles rather than being built into one.

what I worked on

I split my time between the mechanical and electrical sides of the system. On the mechanical side, I worked on the four-gear transmission that steps up the wheel's rotation to the DC motor, including the pitch-diameter, module, and gear-ratio calculations that set the final transmission ratio. On the electrical side, I worked on wiring and integrating the voltage sensor, RPM encoder, and Arduino display, and on running the physical tests that determined the final gear layout.

iterating under testing

v1 — gear position on the chain

Mounting the drive gear near the rear wheel introduced enough friction loss along the chain to hurt charging performance. Moving it to the midpoint of the pedal-to-wheel chain cut those losses and noticeably improved output.

v2 — RPM sensor placement

A 20-slot encoder glued to the motor gear needed the sensor mounted too close for the available space. Switched to reading RPM off the existing 43-slot gear instead — same accuracy, no extra part, no space conflict.

results

At an 80 RPM pedaling rate (measured at 4.13 V, 1 A), the system fully charges a 12V 7Ah battery in about 20 hours 20 minutes from empty — roughly a third of the power output of a standard wall charger, which was expected for a human-powered source and sufficient for the project's device-charging use case.

KariFury — Autonomous Combat Robot

robotics final project

Team of 2 — with Sam Tawm · Supervised by Dr. Roy Abi Zeid Daou · Robotics, 2025

my roleChassis design + sensor/control logic
toolsSolidWorks, Arduino IDE
size limit30 × 20 cm, 1.5 kg
match format3 min, 2 m ring, autonomous

the problem

Build a fully autonomous sumo-style robot that pushes an opponent out of a 2-meter ring without exceeding tight size and weight limits, then survive real head-to-head matches — which meant the design had to hold up against an unpredictable opponent, not just a test bench.

what I worked on

I contributed to both the mechanical build and the control logic. On the mechanical side, I worked on the upper and lower chassis in SolidWorks and on redesigning the front scraper after the first version came in at 32 cm — over the 30 cm limit — cutting it down while keeping it functional for lifting an opponent's wheels. On the software side, I worked on the boundary-detection and attack-decision logic that reads the infrared and ultrasonic sensors and drives the four motors through the L298N driver.

fig. 01CAD — full assembly (SolidWorks)
fig. 02Video — live match footage

iterating under testing

v1 — minimal sensor set

Started with one IR sensor per side and two ultrasonic sensors. In testing, the robot couldn't reliably detect the ring boundary at an angle, and the ultrasonic sensors kept mistaking the operators' feet for the opponent.

v2 — four IR sensors + repositioned ultrasonics

Added two more IR sensors (four total), which also enabled a new behavior: if KariFury gets shoved backward, the rear sensors trigger an automatic evasive spin instead of the robot reacting too late. Moved both ultrasonic sensors to the front for a wider detection cone.

v3 — distance threshold in software

Added a ~30 cm detection threshold in the control logic to filter out false triggers from distant objects and bystanders' feet, stabilizing the attack-decision behavior for actual matches.

result

A compliant, fully autonomous robot that detects the ring boundary, tracks an opponent by distance, and switches into a full-speed attack — built and rewired through three testing rounds after the first two sensor layouts failed under real match conditions.
